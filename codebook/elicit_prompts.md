# Elicit extraction prompts

The instructions given to Elicit for the eleven extraction columns, verbatim, as last used in the review. Each column was asked of every article read in full; Elicit also generated, for every column, a supporting-quotes and a reasoning column, which are in `data/elicit/elicit_exports_all.xlsx`.

The columns were run in two tables. Table 1 (identification and aggregation) holds S0, A1, A2, B1, B2 and D1; Table 2 (justification and framing) holds S1, E1, E2, F1 and F2. The code in each heading is the prefix of the column's variables in `data/coded/coded_all.csv` (for example `a1_term`); the name is the column's name in the Elicit export.

## Table 1 — Identification and aggregation

### S0 — Scope

Report only what the paper states. Do not infer from the conventions of the field. Answer with one of the terms below, in capitals, and nothing else. Where the term takes a colon, give at most ten words after it. Do not precede the term with a dash or bullet. These answers are read by software. Does this study operationalise premature mortality — or avoidable, amenable, preventable or excess mortality, or years of life lost — as a population phenomenon? That is, does it apply a rule determining which deaths count, and report a figure for a population? POPULATION — yes. The study identifies premature deaths by some rule and reports a figure for a population, region, group or period. This includes studies where premature mortality is a component of something else, such as years of life lost within disability-adjusted life years, and studies restricted to a single cause of death, provided the deaths are drawn from a population rather than from a clinical setting. CLINICAL: [setting] — the study is a clinical trial, or a cohort drawn from a clinical setting: patients, hospital admissions, a registry of treated cases, participants recruited through care. It addresses efficacy, prognosis, survival after treatment, or in-hospital outcomes in a selected patient group rather than mortality in a population. Name the setting after the colon. NOT OPERATIONALISED: [what the paper is] — the paper uses the term premature mortality or a cognate, but applies no rule identifying which deaths count and reports no population figure: a conceptual, philosophical or historical discussion, a discussion of methods that measures nothing, a study in which the term appears only in framing or motivation. Say what the paper is after the colon. CANNOT DETERMINE — the paper does not give enough to decide. Decision rules: The test is where the deaths come from, not what they are deaths from. A study of deaths from hepatitis C drawn from national vital statistics is POPULATION. A study of deaths among hepatitis C patients at a treatment centre is CLINICAL. Population-based disease registries, vital statistics, census-linked data, and national or regional surveys are POPULATION even where restricted to one cause or one age group. Hospital-based data are CLINICAL unless the study relates the deaths to a population denominator outside the hospital. A study that reports odds ratios, hazard ratios or relative risks for premature death as an outcome, without any population-level figure of premature mortality, is NOT OPERATIONALISED. But answer NOT OPERATIONALISED on this ground only where nothing determines which deaths count. Where the study defines premature mortality for a whole group by its mortality relative to another group — an excess, a survival disadvantage, a ratio between two groups the study itself defines — a rule has been applied and the comparison is that rule, even if the only figure reported is a ratio. That is POPULATION. The question that separates these, and the one to ask of any study reporting a ratio or a coefficient: is a contrast between two groups the study's measure of premature mortality, or is mortality simply the dependent variable? Three shapes recur and they answer differently. A contrast between two groups the study defines, reported as the measure. Deaths in a deprived area against deaths in a non-deprived one; exposed against unexposed; one cohort against the general population. The two terms are two groups' mortality, each of which could be reported as a rate on its own. A rule has been applied — the comparison is the rule — and the answer is POPULATION. A change in the outcome per unit of exposure. Years of life lost per one-degree rise in temperature, deaths per unit of pollutant, a slope from a time-series or distributed-lag model. The two terms are an exposure and an outcome increment, not two populations. Nothing determines which deaths are premature and the answer is NOT OPERATIONALISED. A cohort study in which mortality is the outcome variable. All-cause mortality followed up in a sample, with covariate-adjusted hazard ratios and the association attenuating on adjustment. The study asks what predicts death, not which deaths count as premature. NOT OPERATIONALISED, whatever the paper calls the outcome. Do not decide this from the aim sentence or from the words the paper uses. "Excess mortality", "premature mortality" and "years of life lost" are all used as names for an outcome variable as often as for a quantity. Find the figure and ask what its two terms are. A paper whose contribution is methodological is POPULATION if the measure it applies to real population data is itself a measure of premature, avoidable, amenable, preventable or excess mortality, or of years of life lost. A paper introducing a new index of early death and computing it for several countries has operationalised the concept. A paper applying some other measure — an index of health inequality, a life table method, a model of health determinants — in which deaths enter as an input has not, even where the term premature mortality appears. Only the former is POPULATION. A cohort is CLINICAL only if its members were recruited through care: patients of a clinic, a trial's participants, a treated series assembled by the investigators. A cohort drawn from a general population, a national or regional register, a census-linked file, or a survey sample is POPULATION, even though individuals are followed up and even where the study restricts to people with a given diagnosis. Test: were these people assembled because they were being treated, or were they already a population that the study then followed? An administrative register that records every care contact of a defined geographic population — every hospital discharge in a region, every notified case in a country, every prescription in a national scheme — is a population register, not recruitment through care. That the recorded event is a care contact does not make the register a clinical sample. Test: does every person in the region to whom the event happened appear in it? If so, it is a register and the answer is POPULATION. A study reporting a rate, ratio, count or other figure against a population denominator has operationalised the concept. But a standardised mortality ratio is POPULATION only where the expected deaths come from a general population: national or regional rates, a life table, a census-linked file. Where the referent is a comparison group recruited for the study — matched controls, household members, an unexposed arm — the study is CLINICAL, because numerator and denominator alike were assembled through the research rather than being a population. A study that computes a measure of early or premature death for real populations has operationalised the concept, whatever its own framing. A demographic paper deriving an index of early death and computing it for a set of national life tables is POPULATION, not a methodological discussion. Before answering CLINICAL or NOT OPERATIONALISED, ask whether the study reports any population-level figure of premature, avoidable, amenable or excess mortality, or of years of life lost. If it does, it has operationalised the concept and the answer is POPULATION. This test overrides the cohort rule above: where the two disagree, the reported figure decides. Two studies may both report a standardised mortality ratio and answer differently. Where the expected deaths come from the mortality of the region or country in which the cohort lives, the answer is POPULATION. Where they come from a comparison group the investigators assembled, the answer is CLINICAL. The referent decides, not the cohort. A study that reports a named measure of premature, avoidable, amenable or excess mortality — a premature death rate, years of potential life lost, an avoidable mortality rate, a burden-of-disease estimate — has operationalised the concept even where it never defines that measure and takes it from another source. The definition is inherited, not absent. Answer POPULATION; the identification column records separately that no standard was stated. Historical demography using census or parish data is POPULATION. Prefer CANNOT DETERMINE to a guess. Support your answer with the sentence describing the data source or study population, verbatim after the answer, separated by a vertical bar, with its section heading.

### A1 — Standard type

Report only what the paper states, for the study's headline result. Do not infer from the conventions of the field. Answer in the terms given below: the answer begins with one of them, spelled exactly as given and in capitals. Do not vary the capitalisation, do not precede the term with a dash or bullet, and do not add an explanation the column has not asked for. NOT STATED and CANNOT DETERMINE are given alone, with nothing after them. NOT APPLICABLE is not one of this column's terms and must not be used: every study within scope either applies some basis for treating deaths as premature or gives none, and giving none is NOT STATED. These answers are read by software, and a term spelled three ways is three different answers. On what basis does this study decide which deaths count as premature, avoidable, amenable, preventable or excess, or which deaths contribute years of life lost? Answer with one of the following. ALL DEATHS — every death qualifies, to some degree; no restriction is applied. FIXED AGE — a stipulated age, applied unchanged to every person. DISTRIBUTIONAL STATISTIC — an age derived from the population's own age-at-death distribution: its life expectancy, modal age at death, a disparity age, or a percentile. MODEL COMPONENT — a segment of the age-at-death distribution identified by fitting a statistical model. ATTRIBUTED CONDITIONS — deaths attributed to named diseases, risk factors, exposures or structural conditions, at the level of individual deaths. OTHER POPULATION — the observed mortality of a different population, group, region or country, used as the standard for the study population, where the comparison itself is what makes a death premature. Typical of studies whose measure is a difference in life expectancy, in survival, or in age-standardised mortality between groups, and of studies computing an excess over a set of benchmark countries. A cause list may also be present without changing the answer, provided the cause list alone does not make a death premature. COUNTERFACTUAL SCENARIO — the study population as it would have been under conditions that did not obtain: a continued past trend, a pre-crisis baseline, or the absence of an event, epidemic or exposure. The comparison is with the same population in a state that never occurred. AMENABLE CAUSES — membership of a list of causes held avoidable through health care. PREVENTABLE CAUSES — membership of a list of causes held avoidable through population-level policy. AVOIDABLE CAUSES — membership of a single list held avoidable, where the study does not separate avoidance through health care from avoidance through policy. Typical of the OECD and Eurostat classification, which partitions causes into treatable, preventable, both, and ischaemic heart disease under one heading. The wording varies: "avoidable", "potentially preventable or treatable", "preventable and amenable combined", "avoidable mortality" without further division. Answer AVOIDABLE CAUSES wherever a single list of causes held avoidable is applied and the study does not report separately against a health-care list and a policy list, whatever words it uses; answer AMENABLE CAUSES or PREVENTABLE CAUSES where the study applies one of the two lists alone. Do not answer NEW CATEGORY for a phrasing of this that the terms above do not use verbatim. INTERVENTION — deaths averted by a specified intervention or programme. NOT STATED — the paper gives no basis at all for treating deaths as premature. CANNOT DETERMINE — the paper gives a basis, but does not say enough about it to place it among the terms above. NEW CATEGORY: [description] — the paper states its basis clearly, and it fits none of the terms above. Describe it in at most fifteen words after the colon. Prefer this to forcing a poor fit: a basis assigned to the nearest term it does not belong to is lost, whereas one reported here can be examined. Decision rules: Where a cause list and an age bound are combined, ask first what kind of list it is. Where the list is one of causes held avoidable — an amenable list, a preventable list, a combined avoidable list — membership is itself what makes a death premature, so answer with the cause list; the age bound is a scope restriction inherited from the list and is recorded in B1. Where the list is not an avoidability list but a set of diseases, injuries or exposures naming what the study is about, apply the removal test: if the age bound were removed, would any death still be identified as premature? If not, the age is the criterion and the answer is FIXED AGE, with the cause list recorded in B1. Worked case. A study reporting the probability of dying between ages 30 and 70 from cardiovascular disease, cancer, diabetes or chronic respiratory disease is FIXED AGE. The four conditions are not held avoidable and no list of avoidable causes is applied; the ages make the deaths premature, and removing them leaves nothing identified. B1 records "ages 30 to 70, four major non-communicable diseases" and A2 records COMPOUND. Where the study uses more than one independent rule, answer for the headline one — the rule the abstract and conclusions rest on. The others are recorded in A2. FIXED AGE covers any age applied unchanged to everyone, including one the authors derived from a population's life expectancy. Classify by what is applied, not by how it was obtained. If the threshold would change were the study run on a different population, answer DISTRIBUTIONAL STATISTIC. A study comparing observed deaths with a number expected from its own past trend, or with an estimate of what would have happened had some event not occurred, is COUNTERFACTUAL SCENARIO. Excess mortality studies belong here. Answer OTHER POPULATION only where the standard is the mortality another population actually experienced. COUNTERFACTUAL SCENARIO and ATTRIBUTED CONDITIONS both involve a counterfactual. The difference is what is produced. ATTRIBUTED CONDITIONS assigns particular deaths to a named cause or exposure, so that individual deaths are identified. COUNTERFACTUAL SCENARIO produces an alternative population total, from which the premature deaths are a residual and no particular death is identified. Apply that difference by asking how the deaths were counted, not what they are called. Where the study reads a cause from each death record and counts the records — deaths coded to an ICD chapter, verified deaths in a followed cohort — particular deaths are identified and the answer is ATTRIBUTED CONDITIONS. Where the study runs an exposure-response function, an attributable fraction, a health impact assessment or a comparative risk assessment, and reports the number of deaths that would not have occurred at some counterfactual level of exposure, no particular death is identified and the answer is COUNTERFACTUAL SCENARIO, even where the paper calls the figure attributable. The words "attributable to" appear in both and do not settle it. A paper that describes its own comparison as a counterfactual — "compared to a scenario in which the population is unexposed" — has told you the answer. Answer COUNTERFACTUAL SCENARIO. The converse of the stratification rule below also holds, and matters. Where the study applies no age threshold, no cause list and no life table, and its measure is a difference between populations — a gap in life expectancy, in survival, or in age-standardised mortality — then OTHER POPULATION is the correct answer and the absence of a threshold is not a reason to answer NOT STATED. Answer NOT STATED only where the study gives no basis of any kind for treating deaths as premature. A study whose whole basis is a comparison has given a basis. A study of deaths from a named disease, injury or exposure is not ATTRIBUTED CONDITIONS merely because the disease is named. Ask what makes those deaths premature. If an age, a life table, or a comparison population supplies that, answer with it and treat the disease as the study population rather than as the criterion. Answer ATTRIBUTED CONDITIONS only where the attribution is itself what makes a death premature, with no age or comparison criterion applied. Test: if the cause restriction were removed, would any death still be identified as premature? If yes, the cause is the study population, not the standard. A comparison used to analyse or stratify results is not an identification rule. Where a study identifies premature deaths by an age, a cause list or a life table and then compares the resulting figures across groups, regions, periods or socioeconomic strata, answer with the rule that identifies the deaths, not with the comparison. Test: if the comparison were removed, would any death still be identified as premature? If yes, the comparison is stratification. AMENABLE CAUSES, PREVENTABLE CAUSES and AVOIDABLE CAUSES are exceptions to the rule above, because in those traditions a death may be premature by virtue of the cause being held avoidable, with nothing further required. A study that computes years of life lost against a life table, without applying any age threshold or cause restriction, is ALL DEATHS. Such studies rarely state that every death is premature; they simply assign each death the remaining life expectancy at the age it occurred, which is the same thing. Burden-of-disease and disability-adjusted life-year studies are of this kind: where a paper says that years of life lost are the years lost to premature mortality, and computes them for every death against a standard life expectancy, answer ALL DEATHS and record the table in B2. Do not answer NOT STATED merely because the paper never defines premature mortality in words. Where a cause list appears together with a comparison against another population, ask what the study counts. If every death from a listed cause, within any age cap, is counted as premature, the list is the criterion and the answer is AMENABLE CAUSES, PREVENTABLE CAUSES or AVOIDABLE CAUSES according to which list is applied. If only the deaths exceeding the benchmark population's mortality are counted, so that a death from a listed cause occurring at the benchmark rate is not premature, the comparison is the criterion and the answer is OTHER POPULATION. The list then restricts which deaths are eligible and is recorded in B1. The same question distinguishes the two traditions of amenable mortality research. Studies following Nolte and McKee count deaths from listed causes below an age cap: the answer is AMENABLE CAUSES. Studies following Kruk and colleagues compute an excess over benchmark countries within listed conditions: the answer is OTHER POPULATION. A study applying several standards of the same kind, one after another, has not used a new kind of standard. Alternative age thresholds, a threshold reported alongside a life-expectancy-derived age, a set of cut-offs varied in sensitivity analysis: answer for the standard the abstract and conclusions rest on, record the alternatives in B1, and record the structure in A2. Answer NEW CATEGORY only where a single standard is clearly described and fits none of the terms above, never because a study uses more than one of them. Where a study reports a named measure it did not define — a premature death rate taken from a published ranking, years of potential life lost from a statistical agency, an avoidable mortality rate from an established list — answer NOT STATED. Do not look the definition up elsewhere, and do not infer it from how the measure is usually built. That the standard is inherited rather than stated is a finding of the review, and NOT STATED is how it is recorded. But search the whole paper before answering NOT STATED. A study that borrows its measure from a published indicator often reproduces the definition somewhere other than the methods: in an appendix listing the indicator's components, in a table note, in a figure legend, in the model diagram, or in the name of the measure itself. "Premature death (years of potential life lost under 75 years per 100,000 population)", appearing in a table of components, is a stated basis and the answer is FIXED AGE. NOT STATED is for a definition that appears nowhere in the paper, not for one that appears outside the methods section. Support your answer with the sentence that states what you are reporting, not merely a sentence near it. The quotation must be the one a reader could use to verify the answer on its own. Give it verbatim after the answer, separated by a vertical bar, with its section heading. If the paper contains no sentence stating what you are reporting, write NOT STATED, even where the answer seems evident from the surrounding text. Do not substitute a sentence about a related matter, such as which conditions or populations were analysed, for the sentence stating the criterion itself.

### A2 — Structure of the identification rule

Report only what the paper states. Do not infer from the conventions of the field. Answer in the terms given below: the answer begins with SINGLE, COMPOUND or MULTIPLE, in capitals. Only COMPOUND and MULTIPLE take a colon and further text; SINGLE takes none. Do not precede the term with a dash or bullet. These answers are read by software, and a term spelled three ways is three different answers. This column has no absence terms. NOT STATED, NOT APPLICABLE, NONE and CANNOT DETERMINE are not answers here and must not be used. Every study that identifies premature deaths at all does so by some number of criteria, and a study that identifies none has been excluded at the scope column rather than coded here. In particular, do not carry an answer across from the standard-type column: that column may report that a study states no basis, and this column still answers SINGLE, because the question here is how many criteria are used and not whether they are stated. Where the number of criteria is genuinely unclear, answer SINGLE, as the gate below instructs. This column has a gate. Read STEP 1 before anything else, and answer it before reading the terms. STEP 1. Does the study report two or more separate figures, each for a different set of deaths, identified by different rules? Count the figures, not the analyses. If the study reports one figure for one set of deaths — however many times that figure is broken down, stratified, re-estimated, or computed for different causes, regions, periods, pollutants, exposure windows, data sources or scenarios — the answer is SINGLE. Write SINGLE, give the citation, and stop. Do not read further. STEP 2. Only where two or more separate figures are reported for different sets of deaths: continue below and decide whether the criteria are conjoined or independent. The gate exists because the commonest error in this column is by a wide margin answering MULTIPLE for one criterion applied repeatedly, and because stating the rule as a principle further down has not been enough. If STEP 1 does not clearly return two figures for two sets of deaths, the answer is SINGLE. Answer this column without reference to what was recorded in A1. That column reports the study's headline way of identifying premature deaths; this column reports how many ways the study uses altogether, and how they relate. Apply the same test as A1 for what counts as a criterion. Count every distinct basis on which the study identifies deaths as premature, avoidable, amenable, preventable or excess, or on which deaths contribute years of life lost, including any reported in secondary analyses, subcomponents or supplementary results. Count only criteria that make a death premature. Do not count restrictions that define whose deaths are studied. Test each restriction by removing it. If deaths would still be identified as premature by some other criterion, the restriction was scope and does not count. If nothing at all would be identified, the restriction was the criterion and does count. Two worked cases. A study of deaths from hepatitis C treating them as premature if they occur before a retirement age of 60 is SINGLE: remove hepatitis C and deaths before 60 are still premature, so the disease is scope; remove the retirement age and nothing is identified, so the age is the criterion. A study restricting to amenable conditions and counting as premature the excess over a group of benchmark countries is also SINGLE: remove the cause list and excess over the benchmark is still identified, so the list is scope; remove the benchmark and nothing is identified, so the comparison is the criterion. Then answer with one of the following. SINGLE — one criterion identifies the premature deaths. Answer the single word SINGLE and nothing further: no dash, no explanation, no restatement of the criterion, which is already recorded in A1 and B1. COMPOUND: [criteria] — two or more criteria that must all hold together to identify a single set of deaths, yielding one figure. Name the criteria after the colon, in at most ten words. Example: "COMPOUND: amenable cause list and age under 75". A death qualifies only if it satisfies every criterion. MULTIPLE: [second rule] — two or more criteria applied independently of one another, each identifying its own set of deaths and yielding its own figure. Name the criteria other than the headline one after the colon, in at most fifteen words. Example: "MULTIPLE: deaths under age 1, reported alongside deaths under 65". A death may satisfy one criterion without satisfying the others. Decision rules: The test separating COMPOUND from MULTIPLE is whether the criteria are conjoined or independent. Conjoined criteria narrow one set of deaths and produce one figure. Independent criteria produce separate figures that are reported alongside each other. Reporting the same set of deaths as both a count and a total of years of life lost is SINGLE, not MULTIPLE. One criterion identified the deaths; two figures were computed from them. That case is recorded in D1. Reporting the same measure separately by sex, region, period or socioeconomic group is SINGLE. Stratifying results does not add a criterion. A measure described as a subcomponent, a sensitivity analysis, or an additional outcome is MULTIPLE only if it identifies a different set of deaths. Decomposing one set of premature deaths into components is not MULTIPLE. A study that identifies amenable deaths and then partitions them by whether care was of poor quality or was not sought has one criterion; the components are a breakdown of the same deaths, not separate identifications. The same applies to breakdowns by cause, sex, region, or contributing factor. Test: do the components sum to the headline figure? If so, they are a decomposition and the answer is SINGLE for that part. Preventable and amenable mortality reported as components of one excess are a decomposition, not two cause lists: they sum to the excess, and the answer is SINGLE. The same holds where amenable mortality is further split into deaths from poor quality of care and deaths from non-utilisation. Answer MULTIPLE only where two lists are applied independently to the whole population, each producing a figure that does not sum with the other. Sensitivity analyses varying a parameter of the same criterion — the same threshold at 65 and at 75 — are SINGLE. Record the alternatives in B1. A disease, injury, exposure, occupation, region or birth cohort naming whose deaths are studied is not a criterion. Cause-specific mortality studies are SINGLE unless a second criterion of prematureness is applied. An observation window is not a criterion. Death by the end of follow-up, death during the study period, death within a stated number of years of baseline: these say which deaths were observed, not which are premature. A study defining premature death as death before 65 and also reporting death by the follow-up date has one criterion, not two. Answer SINGLE. Apply the removal test: remove the follow-up window and deaths before 65 are still premature, so the window was scope. Amenable and preventable cause lists are exceptions: there the cause is itself the criterion of prematureness, so a list combined with an age cap is COMPOUND. Applying one criterion several times is not MULTIPLE. A study that attributes deaths to two pollutants, to hot and to cold spells, to several risk factors, or to short-term and long-term exposure windows, has one criterion — attribution — applied repeatedly. The same holds for a study reporting a measure for several causes, several regions, or several years. Answer SINGLE and record the criterion once. Alternative estimates of the same quantity are not MULTIPLE. Two data sources giving different figures, three counterfactual scenarios, an upper and a lower bound, or a main and a sensitivity estimate are alternative values of one criterion, not separate criteria. Answer SINGLE. Components of a composite measure are not MULTIPLE. Disability-adjusted life years are the sum of years of life lost and years lived with disability; the mortality component is one criterion. Answer SINGLE. Answer MULTIPLE only where a second criterion identifies a set of deaths that the first does not, and the study reports a separate figure for it. Deaths under 65 reported alongside deaths under 1 is MULTIPLE: the infant deaths are identified by a different age. If in doubt, answer SINGLE and record the secondary measure in the figure column. Before answering COMPOUND or MULTIPLE, find the sentence that states the second criterion. It must be a sentence a reader could use, on its own, to see that the study identifies a further set of deaths as premature by a different rule. A section heading is not such a sentence. A sentence saying that two analyses were performed, that results are reported separately, or that estimates should not be added together, is not such a sentence either: it describes how results are presented, not a second criterion of prematureness. If no sentence states a second criterion, the second criterion has not been found and the answer is SINGLE. Support your answer with that sentence, not merely a sentence near it. Give it verbatim after the answer, separated by a vertical bar, with its section heading. Where the answer is SINGLE, give the word SINGLE, a vertical bar, and the sentence stating the criterion that identifies the deaths, with no other text.

### B1 — Specification of the standard

Report only what the paper states, for the study's headline result. Do not infer from the conventions of the field. Answer with the content of the standard only, in the paper's own words where possible. Do not label or classify it, do not precede it with a dash or bullet, and do not add commentary. NOT STATED is given alone. Specify exactly the standard by which the study decides which deaths are premature, avoidable, amenable, preventable or excess, or against which years of life lost are counted. Name it exactly as the paper states it, in at most fifteen words. Where the paper's own wording is longer, keep the identifying detail — the number, the name, the year — and drop the rest. Do not classify the standard: its type is recorded elsewhere. Give only its content — the age, the statistic, the list, the population, the counterfactual, the intervention. Examples of well-formed answers: "under age 65" "ages 30 to 70 years" "the assumed retirement age of 60 years" "modal age at death, Irish females 1841" "Nolte and McKee amenable conditions 2011, ages under 75" "23 high-income countries scoring over 90 on the Universal Health Coverage index" "the Famine did not occur; pre-Famine rates of 1821-1841 projected forward" "monthly mortality expected from the 2015-2019 average" "the GBD standard life expectancy at the age of death" "all deaths, with no age or cause restriction" Rules: Answer for the standard that decides which deaths are premature, not for every number in the paper. Policy targets, sample inclusion criteria, model parameters, reporting age groups and data sources are not the standard. Test: would a death fall outside the premature count if it fell outside this value, or merely outside the sample? Report it only in the first case. Where a cause list is combined with an age cap, give both. Where the standard is a counterfactual, give what is assumed not to have happened, the years the expectation is built from, and how it was produced, as far as the paper states them. A population whose data are used to construct a counterfactual is not the standard. Where a study builds an expected mortality for its own population and borrows another country's rates as an input, describe the counterfactual and name the borrowed source within it. Where a life table is used to compute years of life lost rather than to identify premature deaths, it is still the standard against which the years are counted: name it. Do not convert, round or standardise. Report age 74 as age 74, not "about 75". If a life table or cause list is named, give its name and year or version. If the study uses several standards, give the one used in the headline result. Give whatever the paper states and omit what it does not. An incomplete specification is more useful than none. Write NOT STATED alone only where the paper specifies no standard at all. Support your answer with the sentence that states the standard, not merely a sentence near it. Give it verbatim after the answer, separated by a vertical bar, with its section heading. If the paper contains no sentence stating the standard, write NOT STATED, even where it seems evident from the surrounding text.

### B2 — Life table type

Report only what the paper states. Do not infer from the conventions of the field. Begin the answer with one of the terms below in capitals. OBSERVED, ASPIRATIONAL, MODELLED, BOTH, NEW CATEGORY and CANNOT DETERMINE are followed by a colon and the table's name and use. NOT APPLICABLE is given alone, with no colon, no parenthesis and no explanation. Do not precede the term with a dash or bullet. These answers are read by software, and a term spelled three ways is three different answers. Does the study use a life table or a set of life expectancies anywhere in its calculation — to identify which deaths are premature, to compute years of life lost, or for any other purpose? If so, is it an observed table for a real population, or a standard, reference or aspirational table constructed independently of any single population? OBSERVED — a national, regional or subnational life table for an actual population in an actual period. ASPIRATIONAL — a standard or reference table not describing any one population's observed mortality, such as the GBD reference life table or standard life expectancy, a best-attained or synthetic table, or a normative standard. MODELLED — a life table constructed for a population whose own mortality was not directly observed: a table fitted to fragmentary or historical data, a Coale–Demeny or United Nations regional model life table, or a table estimated by projection or by matching to a model family. It describes an actual population, but as reconstructed rather than as recorded. BOTH — the study uses more than one kind, for different purposes. Name each and its use. CANNOT DETERMINE — a life table is used but the paper does not say which kind. NEW CATEGORY: [description] — a life table or set of life expectancies is used and is clearly neither observed nor aspirational as defined above. Describe it in at most fifteen words. Prefer this to forcing a poor fit. NOT APPLICABLE — no life table or life expectancy is used anywhere. Answer the two words NOT APPLICABLE and nothing else. This distinction is the one on which the review's analysis turns, and it must be recorded wherever a life table appears, not only where it supplies the identification rule. A study that identifies premature deaths by comparison with benchmark countries and then computes years of life lost against the GBD standard life expectancy answers "ASPIRATIONAL: GBD standard life expectancy, used for years of life lost". A life expectancy or life table that enters the analysis only as an explanatory variable — a covariate in a regression, a predictor, a control, a country characteristic — is not doing any work on which deaths are premature or how much is lost, and the answer is NOT APPLICABLE. Answer for tables that identify premature deaths or against which years are counted, not for every appearance of life expectancy in the paper. Where the paper names a table without describing it, answer from the name if it is unambiguous — the GBD reference or standard life expectancy is ASPIRATIONAL, a national period life table is OBSERVED — and CANNOT DETERMINE otherwise. Support your answer with the sentence naming the life table, not merely a sentence near it. Give it verbatim after the answer, separated by a vertical bar, with its section heading.

### D1 — Form of the reported quantity

Report only what the paper states. Do not infer from the conventions of the field. Answer in the terms given below, spelled exactly as given and in capitals. Where more than one figure is reported, separate the terms with semicolons. Do not precede a term with a dash or bullet, and do not add an explanation the column has not asked for. CANNOT DETERMINE is given alone. These answers are read by software, and a term spelled three ways is three different answers. List every population-level figure the study presents as its measure of premature, avoidable, amenable, preventable or excess mortality, or of years of life lost. The order matters and is part of the answer. Give first the figure the abstract and the conclusions rely on — the one the study's main claims are stated in — then the others, separated by semicolons. Where a study computes more than one figure and its conclusions rest on only one of them, the ordering records which. Where two are relied on equally, give the one appearing first in the abstract. COUNT — a number of deaths. RATE — a crude or age-standardised death rate. RATIO — a standardised mortality ratio or similar ratio of deaths. PROBABILITY — a probability of dying between two specified ages, reported for a population, such as the probability of dying between exact ages 30 and 70. YEARS TOTAL — a total of years of life lost, unweighted. YEARS RATE — years of life lost per unit of population, unweighted. YEARS PER DEATH — years of life lost divided by the number of deaths: the average years lost per decedent, rather than a total or a figure per unit of population. WEIGHTED YEARS — years of life lost with discounting or age weighting applied. Give the discount rate and any age-weighting parameters after a colon. LIFE EXPECTANCY — a level of, or difference in, life expectancy in years. RELATIVE RISK — a ratio or difference between the mortality of two groups, reported as the study's measure of how much more premature mortality one group has than the other: a rate ratio, a mortality risk ratio, a hazard ratio between groups, a relative survival advantage, an excess expressed as a multiple, or the same comparison expressed in absolute terms — a difference in rates between two groups, an absolute inequality difference, a slope index of inequality. Relative and absolute forms of the same between-group comparison are one term: what matters is that the figure is a contrast between two groups' mortality, not the arithmetic used to express it. Give the two groups after a colon — "RELATIVE RISK: first homeless after 50 against first homeless earlier". MONETARY VALUE — the mortality expressed in money: a welfare cost, a cost of illness, a productivity loss, a value of statistical life calculation, or such a figure given as a share of gross domestic product. Give after a colon what was valued and by what method — "MONETARY VALUE: welfare cost of homicides, marginal willingness to pay, as % of GDP". PROPORTION — the measure expressed as a share rather than a level: a percentage of deaths that were premature, avoidable or in excess, a population attributable fraction, a ratio of observed to expected deaths, or a difference between two such shares. Give what the share is of, after a colon. RANK — the population's position in an ordering of populations rather than a level of mortality: a rank, a quartile, decile or other banding, a league-table placing, or an index score derived from one. Give what the ranking is within, after a colon — "RANK: quartile of US counties within each state". DISPERSION — a measure of variability or disparity in age at death or length of life, such as e-dagger, lifespan variance, or the interquartile range of ages at death. NEW CATEGORY: [description] — the study reports a figure fitting none of the terms above. Describe it in at most ten words. Prefer this to forcing a poor fit. CANNOT DETERMINE — the paper reports no population figure. Examples: "RATE" for a study reporting only an age-standardised death rate; "COUNT; LIFE EXPECTANCY" for a study reporting excess deaths and expectation of life; "RATE; YEARS RATE" for a study reporting amenable death rates and years of life lost per 100,000. Decision rules: Each form appears at most once. A study reporting deaths for three periods and four regions reports COUNT once, not twelve times. The list records which forms are used, not how many figures of each form appear. Comparisons between groups are not forms. A difference or ratio between two populations' rates, or between two periods, is the analysis performed on the figure, not a further figure. List the form being compared — RATE — and not the comparison. RATIO is reserved for a population's own observed-to-expected ratio, such as a standardised mortality ratio, not for ratios between groups. Report the form of the figure, not how it was obtained. Excess deaths computed by subtracting expected from observed are COUNT. The distinction between COUNT, RATE and PROPORTION is what the figure is divided by. A number of deaths is COUNT. Deaths per unit of population is RATE. Deaths as a share of all deaths, or of the deaths expected, is PROPORTION. A standardised mortality ratio is RATIO; a percentage excess above expected is PROPORTION. A rate is RATE whether crude or standardised. Where the figure is standardised, the supporting quotation should be the sentence naming the standard population, so that it is recoverable without a separate column. Report a figure only if the study presents it as a measure of premature, avoidable, amenable or excess mortality, or of years of life lost. Incidence, prevalence, case fatality rates and utilisation rates are inputs, not measures of premature mortality, and are not listed. List only figures this study computed. A figure the paper attributes to another study — a share, a rate or a total quoted from the literature in the introduction or the discussion, with a citation — is not this study's figure, however precisely it is reported. Check whose figure the sentence is before listing it. If the paper's only such figure belongs to someone else, answer CANNOT DETERMINE. A count of deaths observed in a cohort is not, on its own, a figure of premature mortality. "327 participants died during follow-up", "3194 deaths before age 75 were recorded", "all-cause mortality was used as the outcome measure": these describe how many deaths entered the analysis, not how much premature mortality the population has. List COUNT only where the study presents the number as its measure — excess deaths, avoidable deaths, deaths before a stipulated age reported as a population total. Where the deaths are the input to a hazard model and no population figure is reported, answer CANNOT DETERMINE. RANK is the answer where the study reports where a population stands relative to others and not how much premature mortality it has. A study reporting that a county falls in the worst quartile of a mortality ranking answers RANK, even where the ranking was built from years of potential life lost: the study reports the placing, not the years. Where both the underlying figure and the ranking are reported, list both, headline first. A modelled probability of death for an individual is not a population figure. The dependent variable of a probit, logit or survival model — the probability of dying within a follow-up period, the predicted risk for a person with given characteristics — is a regression outcome, not a measure of how much premature mortality a population has. Answer PROBABILITY only where a probability is reported for a population and is bounded by two stated ages. A measure of inequality in mortality risk or in mortality rates is not DISPERSION. A Gini coefficient or concentration index computed over individuals' predicted risk, or over the mortality rates of socioeconomic groups, describes how unequally mortality is distributed, not how long people lived. DISPERSION is reserved for variability in age at death itself. Do not list an inequality index of risk as a figure of premature mortality. Whether a ratio is a population figure turns on what its two terms are. Ask what is being compared. Where the two terms are the mortality of two groups, each of which could be reported as a rate on its own — deaths in one region against deaths in another, one deprivation stratum against another, one cohort against another — the ratio is the study's measure of premature mortality for the disadvantaged group, and the answer is RELATIVE RISK. Where one term is a reference category in a model of individual risk, adjusted for covariates, and the coefficient describes how an exposure shifts a person's chance of dying, it is a regression output and is not listed. Regression coefficients, odds ratios and hazard ratios of that second kind are not population figures of premature mortality. They describe the association between an exposure and the outcome, not how much premature mortality a group has. Do not list them. If the study reports no population figure and only such coefficients, answer CANNOT DETERMINE. The test is what the ratio is of, not what it is called. A study may call its figure a hazard ratio and still be comparing two groups' mortality; a study may call its figure a relative risk and be reporting a covariate-adjusted coefficient for an exposure. Read the two terms. RELATIVE RISK and RATIO are different. RATIO is a population's own observed-to-expected ratio against a standard, such as a standardised mortality ratio. RELATIVE RISK is a comparison between two groups the study itself defines and reports on. A figure in money is MONETARY VALUE, whatever the arithmetic behind it. Do not reduce it to the mortality figure it was computed from, and do not answer with a rate or a count the paper reports only as background. A study whose headline result is a welfare cost equal to a share of gross domestic product answers MONETARY VALUE first, whatever else it also reports. The denominator distinguishes the three years forms. No denominator is YEARS TOTAL; a population denominator is YEARS RATE; a denominator of deaths is YEARS PER DEATH. A study reporting that each premature death cost an average of 23 years reports YEARS PER DEATH. Do not convert between forms. A study reporting years of life lost per 100,000 is YEARS RATE, not YEARS TOTAL. Disability-adjusted life years are not a form of their own. A study reporting DALYs reports years of life lost as their mortality component: answer YEARS TOTAL or YEARS RATE according to whether a denominator is used, and note DALY after a colon. Answer WEIGHTED YEARS only where the study states that a discount rate or age weights were applied. Years of life lost reported without any mention of weighting are YEARS TOTAL or YEARS RATE; absence of weighting is the norm in this literature and is not itself worth recording. Support your answer with the sentence defining the figure listed first, not merely a sentence near it. Give it verbatim after the answer, separated by a vertical bar, with its section heading. If the paper contains no sentence defining the figure, write NOT STATED.


## Table 2 — Justification and framing

### S1 — Discipline

Report only what the paper shows. Do not infer from the topic alone. Answer with one of the terms below, in capitals, and nothing else after it except the citation. Do not precede the term with a dash or bullet. NEW CATEGORY takes a colon and a description. These answers are read by software. To which discipline does this study belong? Judge from the journal, the authors' affiliations, the framing of the introduction, the data source, and above all the statistics reported. DEMOGRAPHY — the study population is a whole cohort, real or synthetic, or subpopulations of one; the statistics are life table quantities, the distribution of deaths, life expectancy or its decomposition; the data are vital registration, censuses or national statistics; the aim is to measure and compare mortality levels, trends and components at population level. EPIDEMIOLOGY — the study population is exposed and unexposed groups, or a population-based prevalent cohort; the statistics are incidence, prevalence, relative risk or attributable fraction; the data are disease registries or surveillance systems; the aim is to quantify the effect of an exposure, disease or risk factor on mortality. Framing cues: "incidence rate", "prevalence", "risk factor". SOCIAL EPIDEMIOLOGY — the study population is subgroups defined by socioeconomic status, education, ethnicity or neighbourhood; the statistics are mortality inequalities, gradients, or attributable fractions for social factors; the aim is to document and explain social inequalities in premature mortality. PUBLIC HEALTH — the study population is a national or regional population, or administrative subpopulations; the statistics are premature mortality rates, years of potential life lost, or amenable and preventable mortality used as indicators; the aim is to monitor population health and evaluate health systems or prevention policies. Framing cues: "health indicator", "monitoring trends", "amenable mortality", "public health target". GLOBAL HEALTH — the study population is a synthetic global or regional population, or a multi-country comparison; the statistics are years of life lost, disability-adjusted life years, or burden of disease; the data are Global Burden of Disease estimates or WHO databases; the aim is to compare premature mortality across countries and assess global health priorities. Framing cues: "global burden", "DALY". ECONOMICS — the study population is working-age individuals, the labour force, or national subgroups; the statistics are productivity loss, cost of illness, working life expectancy, or the value of a statistical life; the aim is to evaluate the economic cost of premature mortality or the benefit of prevention. Framing cues: "economic burden", "cost-of-illness", "productivity loss", "value of statistical life". PHILOSOPHY — the study argues about the concept, its definition, or its ethical basis rather than measuring it; the journal is one of philosophy, ethics or the philosophy of science. CLINICAL RESEARCH — patients randomised to an intervention against placebo or standard care; the statistics are survival time, hazard ratio, treatment effect or prognosis; the data are trial datasets or hospital recruitment; the aim is to evaluate whether an intervention improves survival. OBSERVATIONAL MEDICINE — patients defined by diagnosis, hospital admission, treatment or registry; the statistics are mortality rate or survival after diagnosis; the data are hospital records or clinical registries; the aim is to describe prognosis and survival in patient groups without comparison to a reference population. NEW CATEGORY: [description] — the study clearly belongs to a discipline fitting none of the terms above. Describe it in at most eight words. Prefer this to forcing a poor fit. Decision rules: Judge from what the study does, not what it is about. A study of cancer mortality using vital registration and life tables is DEMOGRAPHY; one using a hospital registry and survival analysis is OBSERVATIONAL MEDICINE. The statistics reported are the strongest cue, then the data source, then the journal. Author affiliations are the weakest, since departments are mixed. SOCIAL EPIDEMIOLOGY is preferred to EPIDEMIOLOGY where the exposure of interest is a social position — income, education, occupation, ethnicity, neighbourhood — rather than a biological or behavioural one. PUBLIC HEALTH is preferred to GLOBAL HEALTH where the population is one country or its regions; GLOBAL HEALTH where the comparison is between countries or uses Global Burden of Disease estimates. ECONOMICS requires that a monetary or productivity quantity appear. A study using a retirement-age threshold is not ECONOMICS merely because retirement is mentioned. CLINICAL RESEARCH and OBSERVATIONAL MEDICINE are outside the scope of the review. Answering with either should coincide with CLINICAL in the scope column; where it does not, the discrepancy is worth examining. Where a study spans two disciplines, give the one whose statistics it reports. Support your answer with the sentence that best shows the discipline — the aim, the data source, or the definition of the statistic — verbatim after the answer, separated by a vertical bar, with its section heading.

### E2 — Reason for the age threshold

### F1 — Attribution of responsibility

Report only what the paper states. Do not infer from the conventions of the field. Answer in the terms given below, spelled exactly as given and in capitals. Where more than one applies, separate them with semicolons, most prominent first. Do not precede a term with a dash or bullet. NONE and NO AGENT NAMED are given alone. NEW CATEGORY: [description] — the paper attributes responsibility to an agent fitting none of the terms above. Describe it in at most fifteen words. Prefer this to forcing a poor fit. ### F2 — Framing classification

Report only what the paper states. Do not infer from the conventions of the field. Answer with one of the three words below, in capitals, then a vertical bar and one quotation as described at the end. Do not precede the word with a dash or bullet, and add no explanation. These answers are read by software. On the basis of the whole paper, and consistently with F1, how does the study treat its measure? DESCRIPTIVE — the quantity is reported and never treated as better or worse, and the deaths are never treated as things that should have been prevented. EVALUATIVE — the paper treats a lower level as better or a higher level as worse, but does not claim the deaths should have been prevented. NORMATIVE — the paper treats the deaths as things that should have been or should be prevented, whether or not it also treats the level as better or worse. Decision rules: A recommendation to reduce the measure is NORMATIVE even if no agent is named. But the recommendation must be directed at the mortality the study measured. A recommendation about something else the study touched — a workforce, a data source, a research agenda, a policy domain the study correlated with mortality — does not make the paper NORMATIVE, whatever its grammatical force. Apply the same test as F1: is the paper saying someone should act on these deaths, or on something else? Describing deaths as avoidable or preventable in the paper's own voice is NORMATIVE. Using such a phrase only as the name of an established indicator is not, on its own, sufficient; look for whether the paper treats the deaths as things that ought not to have happened. Explaining what caused the deaths is EVALUATIVE, not NORMATIVE. Identifying a determinant, a mechanism or a driver says why the deaths occurred, not that anyone ought to have prevented them. Calling a level high, alarming, unacceptable, perverse, or a burden is EVALUATIVE. So is monetising it: putting a welfare cost on deaths treats them as bad without claiming anyone should have prevented them. Reporting that a level fell and describing this as an improvement is EVALUATIVE. DESCRIPTIVE requires that nothing of either kind appears anywhere in the paper, including the abstract and the final paragraph. Prefer EVALUATIVE to DESCRIPTIVE where there is any doubt. Answer with the single word and nothing else. This column asks for no quotation.
